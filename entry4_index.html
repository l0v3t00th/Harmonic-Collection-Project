Schizophrenia

Schizophrenia is a severe and chronic mental disorder that affects how a person thinks, feels, and perceives reality. It is characterized by distorted perceptions (hallucinations), false beliefs (delusions), disorganized thinking and speech, blunted emotions, and difficulties in social functioning. People with schizophrenia may experience episodes where they lose touch with reality—commonly referred to as psychosis—during which their thoughts and perceptions become fragmented or confused. The disorder typically emerges in late adolescence or early adulthood and is influenced by a complex interplay of genetic vulnerability, neurobiological factors, and environmental stressors. Importantly, schizophrenia does not equate to "split personality" or "multiple selves"—a widespread but false belief. Instead, it is a disorder of perception and cognition, not identity.
The common mistakes surrounding schizophrenia stem from these portrayals. One of the most pervasive errors is confusing schizophrenia with dissociative identity disorder (DID), often called “split personality.” Schizophrenia does not involve multiple identities but rather a fragmentation of perception and thought. Another common misconception is that people with schizophrenia are inherently violent. In reality, they are far more likely to be victims of violence than perpetrators. The majority of individuals with schizophrenia pose no threat to others and struggle instead with fear, confusion, and isolation. Society’s fear of them arises not from fact, but from fiction—from decades of media linking “madness” with danger.
People with schizophrenia often appear disconnected from others, but this disconnection is rarely a matter of choice. The condition affects the brain’s ability to interpret sensory input and social cues accurately. Hallucinations or delusions can distort how others are perceived—voices may whisper that one is being watched, or delusions may convince someone that others intend harm. This leads to mistrust and withdrawal as a form of self-protection. Furthermore, negative symptoms such as emotional flatness, lack of motivation, and difficulty expressing feelings can make social connection exhausting or impossible, even when the desire for connection remains. The result is a painful paradox: the more a person retreats to protect themselves from confusion or fear, the more isolated they become, reinforcing the cycle of loneliness.
